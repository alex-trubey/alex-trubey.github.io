We study a two-sided information market in which suppliers choose content quality and consumers choose whom to follow: how intensely to subscribe to suppliers directly, and how intensely to follow peers who reshare. Quality is sustained by an audience premium: high-quality stories spread further, so low-cost suppliers invest. Platform verification tools (fact-check labels, source badges) make each share more diagnostic of quality and always raise equilibrium quality when the network is held fixed. Once consumers adjust their links, the same policy lowers it: at our calibrated economy, a dose that raises quality by 0.025 on the frozen network lowers it by 0.054 along the equilibrium path. Verification makes peer evidence more convincing, which also strengthens the option of acting on peer evidence without subscribing at all: past a critical dose, following becomes self-reinforcing, the subscription-heavy equilibrium disappears in a fold, and the market tips to a free-riding configuration in which the audience premium for quality is negligible. The reversal extends beyond verification: throttling peer transmission triggers the same collapse at matched strength, so quality-improving policies near the tipping point self-defeat as a class. Two further results characterize the endogenous architecture: supplier-link cost asymmetry generates a core-periphery peer layer with competing routing and capacity effects on quality, and quality peaks at intermediate connectedness even though connectedness is chosen, not fixed.
